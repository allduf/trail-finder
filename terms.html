Trails, roads and land

  • Maps come from OpenStreetMap, governments, clubs, resorts and riders. They can be wrong, old or missing things.
  • A trail on the map isn't permission to use it. Trails may be closed, private, on First Nations land, in a park with its own rules, or not built for bikes. Unofficial trails are shown as plain paths and may not be allowed. Follow signs and land managers.
  • Difficulty, loam, snow, weather and trail reports are estimates. Conditions change fast.

Avalanches and winter travel

Avalanche danger, slope shading and route checks come from Avalanche Canada (and the Japan Avalanche Network in Japan) and from our own reading of the terrain. They don't replace avalanche training, the official forecast at avalanche.ca, your own judgment on the slope, or the gear (transceiver, probe, shovel) and a partner. Slackcountry and backcountry areas are outside ski patrol.

Backcountry routes and avalanche tools

Every backcountry tool in the app, now or added later, is a computer's estimate to help you plan. None of them tells you a slope or a route is safe, and none of them makes the decision for you. Some examples:

  • Less exposed way and Plan a tour. The app picks a line by weighing slope angle, aspect, time spent on steep slopes, the shape of the ground around them, the regional danger rating and the weather. How much each one counts is our judgment from avalanche course rules of thumb, not measured odds. Its steps, Why this way and Watch out come from the same model. You turn it on for each tour. It draws no way when the danger is High or Extreme or when there's no forecast, and that doesn't mean other days are safe.
  • Cabin trip way in. It starts at the hut's winter parking and follows its mapped winter route when the club or guides give one (otherwise the nearest plowed road), and shows the closures and winter access we copied from them. Routes, parking and closures change: check the hut's own page and the park's notices before you go.
  • Terrain model. It works from a 10 to 30 m elevation map. It can't see cornices, wind loading, small slopes, cliffs, crevasses, tree density, open creeks or today's snowpack.
  • ATES terrain classes. Where a park or Avalanche Canada publishes an official rating, the app shows it as we copied it, and it can be out of date. Everywhere else the class is our own estimate, not an official rating.
  • Danger ratings and trip-planner lights (Normal, Extra caution, Not recommended) follow Avalanche Canada's forecast and Avaluator chart. A forecast covers a whole region and can be late or missing. The light is for planning, not a go or no-go decision on the day.
  • Weather, new snow, wind, rain and warming come from weather models (Open-Meteo and others) and can be wrong, especially in the mountains.
  • Official status (Rogers Pass restricted areas, gondola and lift status, road and trail closures, park notices) is copied from the people who run them and can be late or wrong. Their own sign or website is what counts.
  • GPS tracks, skin tracks, heatmaps and other people's trips show where someone went once. They don't mean it was safe then, or that it's safe now.
  • Alerts (avalanche danger, watched tours, late-trip alerts) can arrive late or not at all. No alert doesn't mean nothing changed.

Hunting and fishing rules

Seasons, zones, limits, no-shooting areas and fishing rules in the app are short summaries to help you plan. The official regulations of your province or territory are what count: read them, and check for closures and changes before you go. Crown land and property lines are approximate. Ask before going on private land.

Navigation and live location

Don't rely only on your phone: batteries die, GPS drifts and there may be no signal. Live location and late alerts need signal and can fail. They aren't a rescue service. In an emergency, call 911.

Itinéraires d’arrière-pays et outils d’avalanche

Chaque outil d’arrière-pays de l’application, actuel ou ajouté plus tard, est une estimation faite par ordinateur pour vous aider à planifier. Aucun ne vous dit qu’une pente ou un itinéraire est sûr, et aucun ne prend la décision à votre place. Quelques exemples :

  • Chemin moins exposé et Planifier une sortie. L’application choisit un tracé en pesant l’angle et l’orientation des pentes, le temps passé sur ou sous des pentes raides, les pièges de terrain, le danger régional et la météo. Le poids de chacun est notre jugement tiré des règles de base des cours d’avalanche, pas des probabilités mesurées. Les étapes, Pourquoi ce chemin et Attention viennent du même modèle. Vous l’activez pour chaque sortie. Il ne trace aucun chemin quand le danger est Fort ou Très fort ou qu’il n’y a pas de bulletin, et cela ne veut pas dire que les autres jours sont sûrs.
  • Accès au refuge (voyage en refuge). Il part du stationnement d’hiver du refuge et suit son itinéraire d’hiver cartographié quand le club ou les guides en donnent un (sinon la route déneigée la plus proche), et montre les fermetures et l’accès en hiver que nous avons copiés d’eux. Les itinéraires, stationnements et fermetures changent : vérifiez la page du refuge et les avis du parc avant de partir.
  • Modèle de terrain. Il utilise une carte d’altitude de 10 à 30 m. Il ne voit pas les corniches, le transport par le vent, les petites pentes, les falaises, les crevasses, la densité des arbres, les ruisseaux ouverts ni le manteau neigeux du jour.
  • Classes de terrain ATES. Là où un parc ou Avalanche Canada publie une cote officielle, l’application la montre telle que nous l’avons copiée, et elle peut être dépassée. Ailleurs, la classe est notre propre estimation, pas une cote officielle.
  • Niveaux de danger et feux du planificateur (Normal, Prudence accrue, Non recommandé) suivent le bulletin d’Avalanche Canada et l’Avaluator. Un bulletin couvre toute une région et peut être en retard ou absent. Le feu sert à planifier, ce n’est pas une décision d’y aller ou non le jour même.
