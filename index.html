    How Trail Finder works

    • New trails: pick an area (or tap Near me) and how new, then tap Find new trails. A trail is new when it was first added to OpenStreetMap in that time.
    • Prime loam now: trails whose dirt is damp right now (Loam index 40 to 59), the best riding.
    • Loam alerts: the 🔔 at the top tells you when a trail on your To ride list, or a new trail from the last month in the regions you picked, is in prime loam. Each one alerts once every time it turns damp. Each kind has its own switch in the 🔔 under Change regions.
    • Tap any trail on the map for its difficulty, weather, loam, how to get there and rider reports.
    • Layers (top right of the map) turns on forest roads, wildfires, logging areas, elevation lines, your rides, the map style and offline maps. The ⛰ 3D button sits right under it.
    • Old vs new photos (in Layers) puts an older satellite photo on the left and a newer one on the right. Drag the ⇆ handle across the map: a trail that appears as you pass over it was cut between the two photos.
    • Colours are like Trailforks: green, blue, black, double black (red) and pro line (orange). Grey isn't rated yet. Dotted lines may be unofficial.
    • Trails come from OpenStreetMap mappers, so details can be missing or out of date. Check local rules and closures before riding.
    All the details

    All data comes from OpenStreetMap. For the riding areas from Vancouver to Calgary, GitHub collects the new trails and trail names several times a day, so searches there load in a second or two without waiting on OpenStreetMap. A trail added today shows up after one of the next updates (with so many areas, each one is refreshed every day or two). Anywhere else, the app asks OpenStreetMap live. The areas are grouped into four regions in the Area menu: Sea to Sky and the Sunshine Coast; the Fraser Valley, Kamloops, the Okanagan and Williams Lake; the Kootenays, Revelstoke and Golden; and Calgary, Bragg Creek, Canmore and Kananaskis, and Banff in Alberta. The empty country between the riding towns isn't collected: use “Search this area” there. Forest Service roads from the BC government and BC recreation sites only cover BC (the Wildfires button covers all of Canada); in Alberta the forest roads, trailheads and parking come from OpenStreetMap only. You can also search any other place, or pan the map and tap “Search this area”. It finds paths and tracks that were first added to the map inside the period you pick.

    Loam in the list

    Each new trail shows its Loam moisture index right now, from the same Open-Meteo soil model as the trail details (trails within about 5 km share one reading). Pick “Prime loam now” in the time row to see every named trail in the area, old or new, whose index is 40 to 59 right now (damp hero dirt). They show as teal dots on the map: tap one to load the whole trail.

    Near me

    Tap “Near me” and pick 5, 10, 25 or 50 km. The app asks your browser for your location (it stays on your device), searches that circle and lists only the new trails within that distance, with how far each one is.

    Click any trail

    Click or tap any path or track on the map, even one that isn't in the results, to see everything about it: length, climb and descent, difficulty, who mapped it and when, and its tags. Zoom in a little first so the click lands on the right trail.

    Weather and Loam moisture index

    Each trail's details show the weather now, a 4-day forecast, recent rain and the Loam moisture index: how wet the top 9 cm of soil is, from 0 (bone dry) to 100 (saturated). Under 20 is dry and dusty, 20 to 40 is tacky, 40 to 60 is damp prime loam, 60 to 80 is wet and muddy, and over 80 is saturated. It comes from Open-Meteo's free weather models for the area around the trail, so it's an estimate, not a reading on the trail itself.

    Trail name search

    The search box looks through every named path, track, bike path and trail route in the whole region, old or new, plus the new trails already found. If you know roughly where the trail is, pick that area in “Where is it?”, or type a town or place, so only the trail names around there are loaded and searched. A typed place works outside Sea to Sky too. Typos and close spellings still match. The first time you use it, it downloads the list of names area by area (closest area first) and saves it in this browser for a week. Pick a result to zoom to the trail and see everything known about it.

    What counts as new

    A way is new when its first version is dated inside the period. Overpass gives every way edited in the last 2 years. For ways with more than one version, the app asks the OpenStreetMap API for version 1 and uses its date. A trail that was only edited (retagged, extended) does not count.

    OpenStreetMap's trail search server (Overpass) is free and shared by everyone. When it says it's overloaded, the app waits and asks again later instead of piling on more requests, and the status line says so. Results are saved in this browser, so repeat searches only ask for what changed.

    One catch: when a mapper splits an old trail into two pieces, the new piece gets a new id and shows up as new. Check the history link in the popup when something looks off.

    MTB tagged

    Any tag starting with mtb (mtb:scale, mtb:scale:imba, mtb:type, mtb=yes, …) or membership in a route=mtb relation. mtb=no is excluded.

    Riding areas where mappers seldom tag bike trails (Fernie, Three Blind Mice, the South Chilcotin…): their named paths count as MTB trails too, from the daily all-trails lists. Paths that are wide, open to motor vehicles or closed to bikes are still left out. Around Gold Bridge and the South Chilcotin, every path that follows a Recreation Sites and Trails BC trail counts as well, named or not: it takes the trail's name when the map has none, and its details show what Recreation Sites and Trails BC says about it. Around Revelstoke, so do the paths along their bike trails (the ones listed for mountain biking, like the Revelstoke Cycling Association's Mount Macpherson and Boulder Mountain trails, Frisby Ridge and Mount Cartier), but not the Nordic ski, snowmobile, dirt bike or hiking trails. A trail they list as closed says so. Trails inside a provincial park say so, with BC Parks' rules for bikes there (no e-bikes in South Chilcotin Mountains Park). The Recreation Sites and Trails BC trails and the park boundaries come from DataBC (Open Government Licence - British Columbia).

    Looks like MTB

    A path or bridleway with no MTB tags that scores 2 points or more:

    • +2 signed for bikes: bicycle=designated
    • +1 bikes allowed: bicycle=yes or permissive
    • +1 natural surface: dirt, earth, ground, mud, rock, roots, woodchips
    • +1 rough: smoothness bad or worse
    • +1 narrow: width under 1.5 m
    • +1 trail_visibility or incline is mapped
    • +1 the name sounds like a bike trail (flow, berm, jump, enduro, downhill, singletrack…)
    • +2 bike park trail: bicycle=customers
    • +2 the mapper's description or note calls it a bike trail (mountain bike, MTB, downhill…)
    • +1 linked to Trailforks (source or website mentions Trailforks)

    MTB trail networks: many bike trails have no bike tags at all. A plain path with no bike tags that runs within 400 m of two or more other MTB-tagged trails (with different names) also counts as Looks like MTB, unless it's graded for hiking (sac_scale), signed for walkers (foot=designated), a ski, golf or lit park path, gravel or boardwalk, open to motor vehicles, or its note says it's for hiking, climbing or motorbikes. This is checked again every time trails or trail lists load, so it keeps up as mappers add trails.

    Skipped: tracks (most are forest roads or ATV, moto and 4x4 tracks), paths 2 m wide or more, paths open to motorbikes, ATVs or other motor vehicles, paved surfaces, bicycle=dismount, and alpine hiking routes (sac_scale alpine).

    Difficulty colours

    Trails are coloured like Trailforks: green, blue, black, double black (red) and pro line (orange). The colour comes from mtb:scale:imba (0-1 green, 2 blue, 3 black, 4 double black, 5 pro line), or else from mtb:scale, which mappers here use the same way (S0-S1 green, S2 blue, S3 black, S4 double black, S5-S6 pro line). When a trail has no rating on the map, the app looks for one elsewhere: official open data that rates bike trails (Sépaq, the City of Ottawa, Utah and Pennsylvania state parks), then a rated piece of the same trail. Failing that, it estimates the level from the slope and draws it dashed, with an “Est.” badge: slope can't see rocks, roots or drops, so the guess is the right level about half the time and within one level 96% of the time. Trailforks ratings can't be copied. Grey means no source and no estimate. The “Trail colours” key on the map switches back to colours by trail type.

    All trails

    “All trails” (on by default) draws every MTB trail in view, old and new: every way with an MTB tag, bike park trails and the trails of MTB routes. Official trails (see below) are bold and always shown, even with no option ticked; trails whose status isn't known are thin, and unofficial trails are left out, as before (tick “Unofficial only” to see them). A list of them is collected from OpenStreetMap every day. The trails in the list (new trails, or prime loam) are drawn thicker on top, with a white edge.

    Draw a square

    Tap “▭ Draw a square to find trails” (under the search), then drag over the map: every MTB trail inside the square shows up on the map and in a list, old or new (trails with no name on the map are listed as “Unnamed trail”), from the same daily list as “All trails” (unofficial ones included, with dots). Tap a name to see the trail's details. “Clear square” removes it.

    Official trails

    A trail counts as official when the map says it's run by someone (operator, e.g. a park, town, trail club or bike park), it's on the Recreation Sites and Trails BC list (ref:FFID), it's mapped as informal=no, or it's signed for bikes (bicycle=designated), and nothing flags it as unofficial. The named trails of the South Chilcotin (South Chilcotin Mountains Park and the Spruce Lake area), the paths around Gold Bridge that follow a Recreation Sites and Trails BC trail, the paths around Revelstoke that follow one of their bike trails, and every MTB trail in Williams Lake count as official too. Many official trails have none of these tags, so “Official only” misses some. Unofficial trails are shown as before.

    Moto and hiking-only trails

    To keep the list about mountain biking, trails that look like dirt-bike, ATV or 4x4 trails, and trails where bikes aren't allowed, are left out of the list. Moto means tags like dirtbike:scale, motorcycle=designated, atv=yes, 4wd_only=yes, an ATV route, or a name like “ATV trail”. Hiking only means bicycle=no, mtb=no, stairs, hard hiking terrain (sac_scale T4 and up), or a signed hiking route with no bike access. A trail with any MTB tag, in an MTB route, or with bicycle=designated is never hidden, even if motorbikes are allowed on it too. The exceptions are a ski trail (piste:type) where bikes are banned, like the ski trails of Gatineau Park, and a trail whose name marks it as ski, snowshoe or hiking, like “Loup-Garou (Ski)” or “Marais (Rando)”, unless it also says “(Vélo)” or is signed open to bikes.

    Unofficial (possibly unsanctioned) trails

    Marked with a dark dotted line and an “Unofficial?” tag. A trail is flagged when mappers tagged it informal=yes, closed it (access=no or private), banned bikes (bicycle=no, mtb=no), or wrote “unsanctioned”, “closed”, “illegal” or similar in its note or description. Turn on “Unofficial only” to see just those. This is a hint from the map data, not the legal status: many unsanctioned trails aren't tagged at all, and some tags are out of date.

    Possible unmapped trails (unverified)

    Purple dotted lines are guesses, not mapped trails. Once a week the site looks at the public GPS traces people upload to OpenStreetMap. Where 2 or more different traces run together for 100 m or more, more than 20 m from any mapped road, path, lift or ski run, it's listed. Only lines most traces ride at bike speed (faster than walking, slower than a car) are shown. The date is the first GPS trace on it. Each one links to the Strava and Trailforks heatmaps so you can compare, and to OpenStreetMap so you can map it if it's real. It may be private land, closed, or a GPS glitch: check before riding.

    Forest roads

    Forest Service Roads and other dirt roads have a white edge and a colour for their status (turn them off with “Forest roads”). Once a week the site collects them from two open sources: the BC government's Forest Tenure roads (DataBC, Open Government Licence - British Columbia), which list every Forest Service Road and road permit with its tenure status, and OpenStreetMap dirt roads with their gate, access and deactivation tags. The colour is the road's condition, like on a backroad map. Green is good condition (an active Forest Service tenure, or mapped as smooth or solid). Yellow is degraded (mapped as bad or partly soft). Red is heavily degraded: very rough, 4x4 only, or a retired tenure that's no longer maintained and often deactivated. Black dashes are impassable: deactivated, washed out or abandoned. Grey dashes mean the condition isn't known. Pink dashes mean closed to public vehicles: gated, private or industrial use only. Each road is one line: where both sources map the same road, what a mapper saw on the ground (OpenStreetMap) wins, and the tenure decides only when OpenStreetMap has no condition for it. Tap a road to see both sources. This is mapped data, not a live closure list: check the BC resource road safety notices before driving.

    With “Forest roads” on, the map also shows, once you zoom in: gates (open, locked or not known), barriers, bridges and bridges out, foot bridges, fords and hydro plants, from OpenStreetMap. Tap any of them for details. The “Logging areas” button shows cut blocks (pending, active or recently logged, and retired in the last 15 years) and areas closed to motor vehicles, from the BC government, on their own. With the button off, the roads stay on the map as plain grey-brown lines.

    Wildfires

    The “Wildfires” button shows active wildfires across Canada (fires that are out are left off). In BC they come from the BC Wildfire Service, with their perimeters, areas closed because of fire (area restriction orders), and evacuation orders and alerts. In Alberta they come from Alberta Wildfire, with Alberta's fire control orders (forest area closures, fire bans, fire restrictions and off-highway vehicle bans; the milder fire advisories are left off) and wildfire evacuations from Alberta Emergency Alert. Everywhere else they come from the agencies' reports gathered by the Canadian Wildland Fire Information System, with rough fire areas seen by satellite next to them. It's refreshed every 3 hours, so always check the BC Wildfire map and EmergencyInfoBC, Alberta wildfire status and Alberta fire bans, or the CWFIS fire map and the local fire agency before heading out.

    Fires outside BC and Alberta: Canadian Forest Service, Canadian Wildland Fire Information System (CWFIS), Natural Resources Canada, Canadian Forest Service, Northern Forestry Centre, Edmonton, Alberta (Open Government Licence – Canada). Alberta: contains information licensed under the Open Government Licence – Alberta.

    Skiing

    Pick “⛷ Skiing” in the sport menu at the top. The map then shows backcountry ski routes and huts instead of the bike trails, with a list of what's in view. Every night the site collects them from OpenStreetMap (ski touring, nordic, snowshoe and snowmobile routes, ski route relations, and alpine and wilderness huts; resort runs are there too but off by default) from BC Recreation Sites and Trails (trails whose name or description says ski, nordic, snowshoe or snowmobile), and from camptocamp.org (ski touring and snowshoe routes that have a drawn line). Tap a route or hut for its details.

    Snowmobile trails (red dashes) are OpenStreetMap ways marked for snowmobiles (piste:type=snowmobile, route=snowmobile, or snowmobile=yes or designated, often forest roads that are sled trails in winter) and BC recreation trails that say snowmobile. The ❄ pins are the snowmobile clubs' staging areas: Powder Mountain (Brandywine), Black Tusk (Brohm Ridge), Pemberton Valley (Rutherford and the Hurley), Bridge River Valley (Gold Bridge) and Burke Mountain. “Snowmobile clubs and grooming” lists each club with its trail pass and its latest grooming report. Every 2 hours the site reads the reports that are on a public web page, when the page's robots.txt allows it. Clubs that only post on Facebook or Instagram get a link instead, because those sites don't allow others to copy their posts.

    “Ski heatmap” shows where people actually go in winter: every week the site counts the public GPS traces uploaded to OpenStreetMap from December to April, per 30 m square, leaving out roads, railways, ferries and lifts. Blue is one trace, then cyan, yellow and orange for the busiest lines. Few people upload traces here, so it's patchy. Strava's and Trailforks' heatmaps don't allow other sites to reuse them. “Your ski tours” draws the tours you imported or recorded in Record ride (My rides): a ride counts as a ski tour when its file says skiing or snowshoeing, its name says ski, skin, splitboard, snowshoe or powder, or it was in winter (November to May) and went above 1300 m. They stay on your device.

    “⚠ Avalanche risk” shades the mountains by Avalanche Canada's forecast, on the flat map and on the 3D map. Only slopes steeper than about 25° are coloured, since that's where avalanches start. Each slope gets the danger rating (Low green, Moderate yellow, Considerable orange, High red, Extreme black) of its elevation band, worked out from its height: below treeline under about 1500 m, alpine above about 1900 m on the coast (1700 m and 2200 m inland, 1900 m and 2400 m in the Rockies). Stripes and a stronger colour mark slopes whose direction (aspect) and elevation are named in the day's avalanche problems. The site checks the forecast every 2 hours. It's an estimate from the forecast and the shape of the terrain, not a terrain rating: always read the full forecast on avalanche.ca and travel with training and rescue gear.
