    How Trail Finder works

    Find mountain bike trails that are new to you, ride them when the dirt is just right, and switch to Skiing or Hunting in the sport menu at the top.

    🚵 MTB

    • Search a trail name, an area or Near me, and pick how new.
    • Prime loam = damp dirt right now, the best riding. The 🔔 can tell you when.
    • Tap a trail for its difficulty, weather, loam and how to get there.
    • ▶ Ride follows you on the map and records the ride. ✎ Plan a route joins the trails you pick into one ride.
    • 📷 tells you which trail a photo was taken on.
    • Colours like Trailforks: green, blue, black, red (double black), orange (pro line). Dotted = unofficial.

    ⛷ Skiing

    • Ski tours, huts, resorts and snowmobile trails, with the avalanche forecast and snow stations.
    • ✎ Plan a tour, then record or follow it. Slope angle shows steep ground.
    • Each tour gets a terrain class (simple, challenging, complex) and, with the day's forecast, a light: Normal caution, Extra caution or Not recommended. 🔔 Watch a tour to hear when it changes.
    • It helps you plan; it doesn't replace avalanche training, the full forecast on avalanche.ca, or what you see on the day.

    🦌 Hunting

    • Hunting zones, open seasons and no-shooting areas (towns in red).
    • Tap the map for the rules and wind there. Save your own spots.

    Everywhere

    • Layers (top right of the map): clouds, smoke, wildfires, forest roads, 3D and more. Offline maps is in the Ride tab on phones (in Layers on computers).
    • On phones, use the tabs at the bottom: Map, Find, Ride, Saved, More.
    • Trails come from OpenStreetMap, so check local rules and closures before you go.
    All the details

    Every feature

    • Search: type a trail name, or pick an area (or tap Near me) and how new. Any lists every trail there, old or new, with when it was added; Month to 2 yr show the new trails. A trail is new when it was first added to OpenStreetMap in that time.
    • Prime loam now: trails whose dirt is damp right now (Loam index 40 to 59), the best riding.
    • Loam alerts: the 🔔 at the top tells you when a trail on your To ride list, or a new trail from the last month in the regions you picked, is in prime loam. Each one alerts once every time it turns damp. Each kind has its own switch in the 🔔 under Change regions.
    • On phones the tabs at the bottom are Map, Find (search by name, new trails, near me, prime loam, from a photo), Ride (record a ride, plan a route, offline maps), Saved (To ride, Ridden, your folders, routes, your rides and trails you added) and More (sport, alerts, account). ← Back at the top of a page, or your phone's back swipe, goes back one step.
    • Tap any trail on the map for its difficulty, weather, loam, how to get there and rider reports.
    • Layers (top right of the map) turns on forest roads, wildfires, logging areas, elevation lines, your rides, the map style and (on computers) offline maps. The ⛰ 3D button sits right under it.
    • ▶ Ride (the Ride tab on phones) is for riding with the map: it follows you, points the arrow the way you're facing, keeps the screen on and records the ride. The strip at the bottom names the trail you're on, how much of it is left and which trails branch off at the next junction. The numbers on top are moving time (stops don't count), distance, climb and speed; Ⅱ Pause stops counting while you take a break. Stop, then Save ride, to give it a title and photos in My rides. Don't lock the phone: a locked phone stops the GPS for websites. Tap 🔒 Pocket instead for a black screen that ignores touches (hold to unlock). If the GPS stops anyway, the missing stretch is filled in along the trails when it fits, and a ride cut off by the phone closing the page can be carried on when you open the map again.
    • ▶ Ride this trail (in a trail's details) draws the trail in purple and follows it in Ride mode: it says how far away it is until you reach it, then how much is left. More than 40 m off it for 15 seconds, the phone buzzes (or beeps on iPhones) and the strip turns red. Riding a one-way trail the wrong way also buzzes. One-way trails have small arrows on the map from zoom 14.
    • ✎ Plan a route (Layers, Tools; the Ride tab on phones) goes one step at a time: tap the trails you want to ride, in your order, then ▶ Ride it. Everything else is under ⋯ More: how to build it (picking trails or tapping points), Pedal or Shuttle, the start, Back to start, Share, GPX, Saved and Clear. Tapping points: tap where you start, then each next point, and the route takes the trails and forest roads that make sense on a bike between them: one-way trails only their way, climbs on roads and climbing trails, descents on the downhill trails. Picking trails (the default): tap the trails you want to ride (tap again to take one out) and the app builds the ride, in the order you tapped them, each in the direction that makes sense (climb the climbing trails, descend the downhill ones, follow one-way tags) and joining them with the easiest climbs. Where the next trail starts partway down the one before, the route leaves it there instead of riding to the bottom and climbing back. Your trails show as a list: use ▲ ▼ (or drag them) to ride one first, and the route follows your order; ↻ Best order finds the order with the least effort. It starts at the lowest end of your trails unless you use 📍 Set start, and comes back to the start unless you turn ↺ Back to start off. Both show the distance, climb and the trails used, with arrows for the way to ride. ⤓ GPX downloads it (with heights) and ▶ Ride it follows it in Ride mode. Where trails and forest roads don't connect, it joins them on streets, paths and bridges, and only the stretch with nothing mapped is a dashed straight line, as short as it can be. 🚲 Pedal rides all of it. 🛻 Shuttle has a 4x4 pickup drive you (on the highway too) up the forest roads as close to your trails as it can get, and you ride from there (and get picked up at the bottom): it drives roads in good, degraded or unknown condition (never heavily degraded, closed to vehicles or impassable; where one starts, the truck stops and you ride from there), and never past a locked gate, a barrier or a bridge that's out. With a locked gate near your start there's no shuttle (you're at the gates) and it says so. The drive shows as a dark line with its own distance. Tap ✕ on a shuttle to ride that part instead (one big run from one trail to the next); 🛻 Shuttle here puts it back. Road status is mapped data, not a live closure list: check the road safety notices before driving. Heights come from terrain tiles; without a connection the route goes by distance. The plan stays on this device until you clear it. The side panel stays open while you plan (🔍 Find trails on phones): open a trail and tap ✎ Add to my ride. 💾 Save keeps a ride under a name, and 📂 Saved (also Saved routes in More) opens, renames or deletes them; signed in, they follow you to any device.
    • 📷 Which trail is this? (the camera button next to Search; on phones, the 📷 next to Search trails on the map, or Which trail is this? in Find) finds a trail from a photo, a screenshot of a post or a video of up to 5 seconds (longer or very big videos are turned away: trim them or add a screenshot). A photo or video that kept the spot where it was taken (most phone camera ones do) goes straight to the trail it was taken on. A screenshot or a video from online has no spot, so the app reads the words in it (trail names, places, hashtags, Strava or Trailforks labels, the caption) and lists the likely trails, best first. Paste the post's caption, or write what you see ("big granite wall behind", "Black Tusk in the back"), and pick the area to help it. Big mountains, rock walls and lakes it knows (the Chief, Black Tusk, Mount Currie, Ha Ling and more) list the trails that can actually see them. When the picture checker is set up, a small copy of the picture also goes to an AI (Google Gemini), which looks at the background, signs and overlays, and to Google's image search to find pages with the same picture. Google may keep pictures sent to its free AI. Each result has YouTube, Instagram, Trailforks and Photos links to compare with other riders' videos. After you open one it asks "Is this the trail?": Yes saves the match (with a small copy of the picture, kept private) so later searches with similar words find it faster. The ✕ on the picture clears it to try another. The first time, the word reader downloads about 7 MB.
    • ☁ Clouds (in Layers, in every sport) works like Windy. Now shows the real clouds from the GOES weather satellites (infrared, every 10 minutes): the whiter, the colder and higher the cloud. Move the slider at the bottom of the map (or ▶ play it) for the forecast over the next 3 days: soft white cloud over a slightly darker map, blue for rain, purple for snow. The line under the slider gives the cloud, rain or snow and where the mountains are in cloud at the map centre. On the 3D map, grey mist on the ground means it sits inside the cloud. 📈 opens an hour-by-hour chart of the cloud at each height above the map centre, with the freezing level (Skiing routes and huts show it for their top and bottom). Satellite from NASA GIBS, forecast from Open-Meteo.
    • 🌫 Smoke (in Layers, in every sport) shows the wildfire smoke near the ground, now and for the next 3 days, from Environment Canada's FireWork smoke forecast: pale grey-blue for a little smoke, tan when it's smoky, red-brown when it's thick. The bar at the bottom says how smoky the map centre is (with the AQHI+ number Health Canada uses) and what it means for a hard ride; “Smoke here, next 3 days” draws the forecast for that spot. With ☁ Clouds on too, both follow the same slider. Shown on the 3D map as well. 🔥 Show the fires turns on the Wildfires layer, to see where the smoke comes from.
    • Old vs new photos (in Layers) puts an older satellite photo on the left and a newer one on the right. Drag the ⇆ handle across the map: a trail that appears as you pass over it was cut between the two photos.
    • Bike parks (in Layers) pins the bike parks, MTB centres, skills and jump parks, and pump and BMX tracks across Canada. Orange pins are lift-served bike parks. Tap one for its website, hours, price and directions.
    • Colours are like Trailforks: green, blue, black, double black (red) and pro line (orange). Grey isn't rated yet. Dotted lines may be unofficial.
    • Trails come from OpenStreetMap mappers, so details can be missing or out of date. Check local rules and closures before riding.

    All data comes from OpenStreetMap. For the riding areas from Vancouver to Calgary, GitHub collects the new trails and trail names several times a day, so searches there load in a second or two without waiting on OpenStreetMap. A trail added today shows up after one of the next updates (with so many areas, each one is refreshed every day or two). Anywhere else, the app asks OpenStreetMap live. The areas are grouped into four regions in the Area menu: Sea to Sky and the Sunshine Coast; the Fraser Valley, Kamloops, the Okanagan and Williams Lake; the Kootenays, Revelstoke and Golden; and Calgary, Bragg Creek, Canmore and Kananaskis, and Banff in Alberta. The empty country between the riding towns isn't collected: use “Search this area” there. Forest Service roads from the BC government and BC recreation sites only cover BC (the Wildfires button covers all of Canada); in Alberta the forest roads, trailheads and parking come from OpenStreetMap only. You can also search any other place, or pan the map and tap “Search this area”. It finds paths and tracks that were first added to the map inside the period you pick.

    Loam in the list

    Each new trail shows its Loam moisture index right now, from the same Open-Meteo soil model as the trail details (trails within about 2 km share one reading, and the trail details use that same reading). Pick “Prime loam now” in the time row to see every named trail in the area, old or new, whose index is 40 to 59 right now (damp hero dirt). They show as teal dots on the map: tap one to load the whole trail.

    Near me

    Tap “Near me” and pick 5, 10, 25 or 50 km. The app asks your browser for your location (it stays on your device), searches that circle and lists only the new trails within that distance, with how far each one is. Find a trail has the same Near me button: it lists every trail, old or new, within that distance, nearest first. Tap level or Unofficial chips to narrow it, and trail name searches stay inside the circle.

    Click any trail

    Click or tap any path or track on the map, even one that isn't in the results, to see everything about it: length, climb and descent, difficulty, who mapped it and when, and its tags. Zoom in a little first so the click lands on the right trail.

    Weather and Loam moisture index

    Each trail's details show the weather now, a 4-day forecast, recent rain and the Loam moisture index: how wet the top 9 cm of soil is, from 0 (bone dry) to 100 (saturated). Under 20 is dry and dusty, 20 to 40 is tacky, 40 to 60 is damp prime loam, 60 to 80 is wet and muddy, and over 80 is saturated. It comes from Open-Meteo's free weather models for the area around the trail, so it's an estimate, not a reading on the trail itself.

    Trail name search

    The search box looks through every named path, track, bike path and trail route in the whole region, old or new, plus the new trails already found. If you know roughly where the trail is, pick that area in “Where is it?”, or type a town or place, so only the trail names around there are loaded and searched. A typed place works outside Sea to Sky too. Typos and close spellings still match. The first time you use it, it downloads the list of names area by area (closest area first) and saves it in this browser for a week. Pick a result to zoom to the trail and see everything known about it.

    What counts as new

    A way is new when its first version is dated inside the period. Overpass gives every way edited in the last 2 years. For ways with more than one version, the app asks the OpenStreetMap API for version 1 and uses its date. A trail that was only edited (retagged, extended) does not count.

    OpenStreetMap's trail search server (Overpass) is free and shared by everyone. When it says it's overloaded, the app waits and asks again later instead of piling on more requests, and the status line says so. Results are saved in this browser, so repeat searches only ask for what changed.

    One catch: when a mapper splits an old trail into two pieces, the new piece gets a new id and shows up as new. Check the history link in the popup when something looks off.

    MTB tagged

    Any tag starting with mtb (mtb:scale, mtb:scale:imba, mtb:type, mtb=yes, …) or membership in a route=mtb relation. mtb=no is excluded.

    Riding areas where mappers seldom tag bike trails (Fernie, Three Blind Mice, the South Chilcotin…): their named paths count as MTB trails too, from the daily all-trails lists. Paths that are wide, open to motor vehicles or closed to bikes are still left out. Around Gold Bridge and the South Chilcotin, every path that follows a Recreation Sites and Trails BC trail counts as well, named or not: it takes the trail's name when the map has none, and its details show what Recreation Sites and Trails BC says about it. Around Revelstoke, so do the paths along their bike trails (the ones listed for mountain biking, like the Revelstoke Cycling Association's Mount Macpherson and Boulder Mountain trails, Frisby Ridge and Mount Cartier), but not the Nordic ski, snowmobile, dirt bike or hiking trails. A trail they list as closed says so. Trails inside a provincial park say so, with BC Parks' rules for bikes there (no e-bikes in South Chilcotin Mountains Park). The Recreation Sites and Trails BC trails and the park boundaries come from DataBC (Open Government Licence - British Columbia).

    Likely MTB (no bike tags)

    A path or bridleway with no MTB tags that scores 2 points or more:

    • +2 signed for bikes: bicycle=designated
    • +1 bikes allowed: bicycle=yes or permissive
    • +1 natural surface: dirt, earth, ground, mud, rock, roots, woodchips
    • +1 rough: smoothness bad or worse
    • +1 narrow: width under 1.5 m
    • +1 trail_visibility or incline is mapped
    • +1 the name sounds like a bike trail (flow, berm, jump, enduro, downhill, singletrack…)
    • +2 bike park trail: bicycle=customers
    • +2 the mapper's description or note calls it a bike trail (mountain bike, MTB, downhill…)
    • +1 linked to Trailforks (source or website mentions Trailforks)

    MTB trail networks: many bike trails have no bike tags at all. A plain path with no bike tags that runs within 400 m of two or more other MTB-tagged trails (with different names) also counts as Likely MTB, unless it's graded for hiking (sac_scale), signed for walkers (foot=designated), a ski, golf or lit park path, gravel or boardwalk, open to motor vehicles, or its note says it's for hiking, climbing or motorbikes. This is checked again every time trails or trail lists load, so it keeps up as mappers add trails.

    Skipped: tracks (most are forest roads or ATV, moto and 4x4 tracks), paths 2 m wide or more, paths open to motorbikes, ATVs or other motor vehicles, paved surfaces, bicycle=dismount, and alpine hiking routes (sac_scale alpine).

    Difficulty colours

    Trails are coloured like Trailforks: green, blue, black, double black (red) and pro line (orange). The colour comes from mtb:scale:imba (0-1 green, 2 blue, 3 black, 4 double black, 5 pro line), or else from mtb:scale, which mappers here use the same way (S0-S1 green, S2 blue, S3 black, S4 double black, S5-S6 pro line). When a trail has no rating on the map, the app looks for one elsewhere: official open data that rates bike trails (the Resort Municipality of Whistler, the Columbia Shuswap Regional District around Golden, Revelstoke and the Shuswap, Sépaq, the City of Ottawa, Utah and Pennsylvania state parks), then a rated piece of the same trail. Failing that, it estimates the level from the slope and draws it dashed, with an “Est.” badge: slope can't see rocks, roots or drops, so the guess is the right level about half the time and within one level 96% of the time. Trailforks ratings can't be copied. Grey means no source and no estimate. The “Trail colours” key on the map switches back to colours by trail type.

    All trails

    “All trails” (on by default) draws every MTB trail in view, old and new: every way with an MTB tag, bike park trails and the trails of MTB routes. Official trails (see below) are bold and always shown, even with no option ticked; trails whose status isn't known are thin, and unofficial trails are left out, as before (tick “Unofficial only” to see them). A list of them is collected from OpenStreetMap every day. The trails in the list (new trails, or prime loam) are drawn thicker on top, with a white edge.

    Draw a square

    Tap “▭ Every trail on screen” (under the search) to list every MTB trail on the map you're looking at, or “or draw a square” and drag over the map: every MTB trail inside the square shows up on the map and in a list, old or new (trails with no name on the map are listed as “Unnamed trail”), from the same daily list as “All trails” (unofficial ones included, with dots). Tap a name to see the trail's details. “Clear square” removes it.

    Official trails

    A trail counts as official when the map says it's run by someone (operator, e.g. a park, town, trail club or bike park), it's on the Recreation Sites and Trails BC list (ref:FFID), it's mapped as informal=no, or it's signed for bikes (bicycle=designated), and nothing flags it as unofficial. The named trails of the South Chilcotin (South Chilcotin Mountains Park and the Spruce Lake area), the paths around Gold Bridge that follow a Recreation Sites and Trails BC trail, the paths around Revelstoke that follow one of their bike trails, and every MTB trail in Williams Lake count as official too. Many official trails have none of these tags, so “Official only” misses some. Unofficial trails are shown as before.

    Moto and hiking-only trails

    To keep the list about mountain biking, trails that look like dirt-bike, ATV or 4x4 trails, and trails where bikes aren't allowed, are left out of the list. Moto means tags like dirtbike:scale, motorcycle=designated, atv=yes, 4wd_only=yes, an ATV route, or a name like “ATV trail”. Hiking only means bicycle=no, mtb=no, stairs, hard hiking terrain (sac_scale T4 and up), or a signed hiking route with no bike access. A trail with any MTB tag, in an MTB route, or with bicycle=designated is never hidden, even if motorbikes are allowed on it too. The exceptions are a ski trail (piste:type) where bikes are banned, like the ski trails of Gatineau Park, and a trail whose name marks it as ski, snowshoe or hiking, like “Loup-Garou (Ski)” or “Marais (Rando)”, unless it also says “(Vélo)” or is signed open to bikes.

    Unofficial trails

    Marked with a dark dotted line and an “Unofficial” tag (hover or long-press it to see why). A trail is flagged when mappers tagged it informal=yes, closed it (access=no or private), banned bikes (bicycle=no, mtb=no), or wrote “unsanctioned”, “closed”, “illegal” or similar in its note or description. Turn on “Unofficial only” to see just those. This is a hint from the map data, not the legal status: many unsanctioned trails aren't tagged at all, and some tags are out of date.

    Possible unmapped trails (unverified)

    Purple dotted lines are guesses, not mapped trails. Once a week the site looks at the public GPS traces people upload to OpenStreetMap. Where 2 or more different traces run together for 100 m or more, more than 20 m from any mapped road, path, lift or ski run, it's listed. Only lines most traces ride at bike speed (faster than walking, slower than a car) are shown. The date is the first GPS trace on it. Each one links to the Strava and Trailforks heatmaps so you can compare, and to OpenStreetMap so you can map it if it's real. It may be private land, closed, or a GPS glitch: check before riding.

    Forest roads

    Forest Service Roads and other dirt roads have a white edge and a colour for their status (turn them off with “Forest roads”). Once a week the site collects them from two open sources: the BC government's Forest Tenure roads (DataBC, Open Government Licence - British Columbia), which list every Forest Service Road and road permit with its tenure status, and OpenStreetMap dirt roads with their gate, access and deactivation tags. The colour is the road's condition, like on a backroad map. Green is good condition (an active Forest Service tenure, or mapped as smooth or solid). Yellow is degraded (mapped as bad or partly soft). Red is heavily degraded: very rough, 4x4 only, or a retired tenure that's no longer maintained and often deactivated. Black dashes are impassable: deactivated, washed out or abandoned. Grey dashes mean the condition isn't known. Pink dashes mean closed to public vehicles: gated, private or industrial use only. Each road is one line: where both sources map the same road, what a mapper saw on the ground (OpenStreetMap) wins, and the tenure decides only when OpenStreetMap has no condition for it. Tap a road to see both sources. This is mapped data, not a live closure list: check the BC resource road safety notices before driving.

    With “Forest roads” on, the map also shows, once you zoom in: gates (open, locked or not known), barriers, bridges and bridges out, foot bridges, fords and hydro plants, from OpenStreetMap. Tap any of them for details. The “Logging areas” button shows cut blocks (pending, active or recently logged, and retired in the last 15 years) and areas closed to motor vehicles, from the BC government, on their own. With the button off, the roads stay on the map as plain grey-brown lines.

    Wildfires

    The “Wildfires” button shows active wildfires across Canada (fires that are out are left off). In BC they come from the BC Wildfire Service, with their perimeters, areas closed because of fire (area restriction orders), and evacuation orders and alerts. In Alberta they come from Alberta Wildfire, with Alberta's fire control orders (forest area closures, fire bans, fire restrictions and off-highway vehicle bans; the milder fire advisories are left off) and wildfire evacuations from Alberta Emergency Alert. Everywhere else they come from the agencies' reports gathered by the Canadian Wildland Fire Information System, with rough fire areas seen by satellite next to them. It's refreshed every 3 hours, so always check the BC Wildfire map and EmergencyInfoBC, Alberta wildfire status and Alberta fire bans, or the CWFIS fire map and the local fire agency before heading out.

    Fires outside BC and Alberta: Canadian Forest Service, Canadian Wildland Fire Information System (CWFIS), Natural Resources Canada, Canadian Forest Service, Northern Forestry Centre, Edmonton, Alberta (Open Government Licence – Canada). Alberta: contains information licensed under the Open Government Licence – Alberta.

    Skiing

    Pick “⛷ Skiing” in the sport menu at the top. The map then shows backcountry ski routes and huts instead of the bike trails. Under the map buttons, a short Avalanche card and a list of what's in view: Routes and Huts, nearest the middle of the map first, each with how far it is, how long and how hard. Type a name in the search box to find a route or hut anywhere. Every night the site collects them from OpenStreetMap (ski touring, nordic, snowshoe and snowmobile routes, ski route relations, and alpine and wilderness huts; resort runs are there too but off by default) from BC Recreation Sites and Trails (trails whose name or description says ski, nordic, snowshoe or snowmobile), and from camptocamp.org (ski touring and snowshoe routes that have a drawn line). Tap a route or hut for its details.

    Four buttons at the top of the panel pick what's on the map: ⛷ Touring (ski touring and ski access routes, other winter routes, huts, camptocamp route starts and snow stations, with the Avalanche card), 🎿 Nordic (nordic and snowshoe trails), 🛷 Snowmobile (snowmobile trails and the clubs with their grooming reports) and 🚡 Resorts (runs, and in-bounds off-piste and bowls). More than one can be on. The Avalanche card shows the day's ratings for the middle of the map (Alpine, Treeline and Below treeline), with the problems, the forecaster's notes and riders' reports one tap away under “Problems, notes and riders' reports”. In Layers on the map, the Skiing part has ⚠ Avalanche risk, Slope angle, Snow stations, the Ski heatmap, Your ski tours, and every single layer under “Single layers”, and 📷 Webcams: live ski resort and backcountry webcams pinned where each camera is (about 100 cams at 21 BC and Alberta resorts, Whistler Peak first; backcountry cams at Kees and Claire Hut, Mount Abbott and Mount Fidelity at Rogers Pass, Boulder Cabin and Sol Mountain Lodge in teal; DriveBC highway cams at the passes where tours start, like Rogers Pass, the Duffey, the Coquihalla and Kootenay Pass, in grey). Tap a pin for the live feed, shown from the owner's own player, with a link to their webcam page. Sledding and tubing hills aren't shown.

    Rogers Pass (Glacier National Park): with the map there, a “Rogers Pass today” card shows which Winter Restricted Areas and parking lots Parks Canada has posted open for the day (artillery avalanche control closes the rest; read from Parks Canada's live map every 15 minutes on winter mornings), the Winter Permit rules, the ACC huts and Parks Canada's official terrain class (ATES) for 50 named runs. The map colours the permit areas: green open today, red closed today, amber not posted yet, blue Unrestricted (a permit is still needed), purple striped Prohibited all winter. Routes there get their official class and which area they go through in their details. Switch it off with “Rogers Pass permit areas” in Layers. Parks Canada's posted order always wins over the map.

    ⛰ Backcountry areas across Canada (button under Plan a tour, and purple ⛰ pins, switch “⛰ Ski areas” in Layers): about a hundred touring areas, hut-to-hut traverses (purple) and expeditions (dark), from the North Shore, Sea to Sky, the Duffey and the Kootenays to the Rockies and Kananaskis, the Yukon, the Chic-Chocs and the rest of Quebec, Gros Morne, the Maritimes and Baffin Island. Each one says how to get there, its huts and lodges, the rules and permits (Rogers Pass winter permits, Kananaskis Conservation Pass, caribou closures in Jasper and the Chic-Chocs), who forecasts avalanches there with a link to today's forecast at that spot, and the official avalanche terrain ratings (ATES: Simple, Challenging, Complex) that Parks Canada and Alberta Parks publish for its named trips, with the sources. The list can be searched and is sorted by distance from the middle of the map.

    A route's details lead with its elevation: top and bottom height, climb and descent, and a height profile (move along it to see the spot on the map; orange is ground of 30° and steeper). Below that: how steep the ground under the route gets, which way its steep parts face and how much of it is below treeline, at treeline and in the alpine; the avalanche forecast for the route's area with the bands it goes through, and which of the day's avalanche problems are on its slopes; avalanches that riders reported within 15 km in the last 14 days on Avalanche Canada's Mountain Information Network, and the forecaster's notes on recent avalanches; the weather at the top (temperature, new snow, wind and freezing level, from Open-Meteo); huts and camptocamp routes close by; the closest snow stations; ▶ Follow this tour; and a GPX download with the heights. Heights come from open terrain data about 12 m apart, so small cliffs don't show. Unnamed routes are named after the closest named peak, like “Ski touring near Ipsoot Mountain”.

    ⚠ Steep slopes above: a route's details also say how much of it runs under ground of 30° or steeper, within 350 m and at least 50 m higher, that could slide onto it, and draw those stretches as red dashes. A valley route can be under avalanche terrain without ever being steep itself. It's worked out from the same terrain data, so it can miss small slopes and doesn't know about trees.

    Avalanche check: every backcountry route has a terrain class, like ATES (Avalanche Terrain Exposure Scale): Simple (green), Challenging (blue) or Complex (black). It's an estimate worked out every night from the shape of the ground: how much of the route is on slopes of 30° and 35° or more, under steep slopes that could slide onto it, or in gullies and creek beds (terrain traps). Forest, cornices and glaciers aren't seen, so where a park has an official rating it's shown instead, and Avalanche Canada's trip planner has the official ATES maps. With the day's danger rating in the bands the route goes through, the class gives the light from Avalanche Canada's Avaluator chart: Normal caution, Extra caution or Not recommended, for each forecast day. The route list shows it, and “Sort: lowest risk” puts the lowest-risk routes first. 🔔 Watch this tour (in a route's details) adds it to the bell: you hear when it goes to Extra caution or Not recommended, and, with “Tell me when the avalanche danger goes up” on in the 🔔, when your regions go to Considerable or more (on your phone too, with 📱 on).

    Avalanche weather: a route's weather, and the Avalanche card for the middle of the map (at alpine height), list the weather signs that make avalanches: heavy new snow (last day and 3 days), a storm coming, wind strong enough to load snow (and which slopes it loads), rain on snow and warming, with the freezing level.

    Slope direction and Runout zones and traps (in Layers): the first colours slopes of 25° and more by the way they face, to find the forecast's problem aspects on the map. The second shades the ground below steep slopes that avalanches from them could reach (a line 24° down from the start zone, up to 1 km, no ridge in between), with terrain traps striped; it shows from zoom 12 and is a rough estimate: big avalanches can run further.

    Slope angle (in Layers) colours the ground by how steep it is, any day, whatever the forecast: yellow 27-29°, orange 30-34°, red 35-39°, purple 40-44° and blue 45° and up. Most avalanches start on slopes from 30 to 45°. It shows from zoom 9 in.

    Snow stations (teal pins) are the BC River Forecast Centre's automated snow weather stations, mostly high in the mountains. Every 2 hours the site reads each one's snow depth, how much it changed in the last 24 and 72 hours (a rise is new snow), the snow water, the air temperature with the last day's low and high, and the snow depth on each of the last 7 days. Tap a pin for the numbers. Data: Open Government Licence - British Columbia.

    ✎ Plan a tour: tap the button, then tap the map to drop points; the line follows your taps and the bar on the map shows the distance, climb and descent so far (↶ Undo takes the last point off). Slope angle turns on while you plan. Tap ✓ Done for the same details as any route: height profile, steepness, avalanche bands, steep slopes above, weather and GPX. Your plan stays on this device until you clear it. ▶ Record a tour starts Ride's recorder in Skiing: the strip at the top shows your height and the slope where you stand, and turns orange on 30° or more. ▶ Follow this tour in a route's details does the same while showing how far you are from the route. Recorded tours are saved as ski tours in My rides.

    Offline: in Skiing, “⤓ Offline maps” (in the Ride tab on phones, the Layers menu on computers) also saves the ski routes, huts, avalanche forecast, snow stations and the terrain data for the area (for heights, the slope angle colours and the strip while you record), so they work with no signal.

    Snowmobile trails (red dashes) are OpenStreetMap ways marked for snowmobiles (piste:type=snowmobile, route=snowmobile, or snowmobile=yes or designated, often forest roads that are sled trails in winter) and BC recreation trails that say snowmobile. The ❄ pins are the snowmobile clubs' staging areas: Powder Mountain (Brandywine), Black Tusk (Brohm Ridge), Pemberton Valley (Rutherford and the Hurley), Bridge River Valley (Gold Bridge) and Burke Mountain. “Snowmobile clubs and grooming” lists each club with its trail pass and its latest grooming report. Every 2 hours the site reads the reports that are on a public web page, when the page's robots.txt allows it. Clubs that only post on Facebook or Instagram get a link instead, because those sites don't allow others to copy their posts.

    “Ski heatmap” shows where people actually go in winter: every week the site counts the public GPS traces uploaded to OpenStreetMap from December to April, per 30 m square, leaving out roads, railways, ferries and lifts. Blue is one trace, then cyan, yellow and orange for the busiest lines. Few people upload traces here, so it's patchy. Strava's and Trailforks' heatmaps don't allow other sites to reuse them. “Your ski tours” draws the tours you imported or recorded with Ride (My rides): a ride counts as a ski tour when it was recorded in Skiing, its file says skiing or snowshoeing, its name says ski, skin, splitboard, snowshoe or powder, or it was in winter (November to May) and went above 1300 m. They stay on your device.

    “⚠ Avalanche risk” shades the mountains by Avalanche Canada's forecast, on the flat map and on the 3D map. Only slopes steeper than about 25° are coloured, since that's where avalanches start. Each slope gets the danger rating (Low green, Moderate yellow, Considerable orange, High red, Extreme black) of its elevation band, worked out from its height: below treeline under about 1500 m, alpine above about 1900 m on the coast (1700 m and 2200 m inland, 1900 m and 2400 m in the Rockies). Stripes and a stronger colour mark slopes whose direction (aspect) and elevation are named in the day's avalanche problems. The site checks the forecast every 2 hours. It's an estimate from the forecast and the shape of the terrain, not a terrain rating: always read the full forecast on avalanche.ca and travel with training and rescue gear.

    Hunting

    Pick “🦌 Hunting” in the sport menu at the top. The bike trails make way for every hunting zone in BC (management units, labelled with the number and, closer in, the game management zone) and Alberta (WMUs with number and name), all drawn as dark lines with a white edge. The buttons at the top of the panel do the main jobs in one tap: My spot (your location), Save a point, Hunting log, Mark my truck, Back to truck (a line from you to the truck with its distance and direction, updated as you walk) and Record walk (saved in My rides, shareable as GPX).

    Tap the map to see that spot: its zone with a link to the regulations, the open seasons there by species (the ones open today marked ● Open, read each month from the BC regional synopses and the Alberta Guide to Hunting Regulations), the BC limited entry hunts in the unit with last year's first-choice odds (shaded when the spot is inside the hunt's zone) or Alberta's special licence draws with this year's tags, applicants and the priority needed, and any park, town, reserve, military land or closure with what it means for hunting (towns link to their firearm discharge bylaw). In BC it also says whether the land is private or Crown, the forest stand (species, age, height) and when it last burned, from DataBC; in Alberta, whether it's a grazing lease (contact the leaseholder), the forest fuel type, burns and wetlands from Alberta's map server.

    The wind card shows where the wind comes from and where your scent goes (the orange cone on the map, wider in light wind), gusts, temperature, cloud and the next 12 hours, from Open-Meteo. The light card has legal light (half an hour before sunrise to half an hour after sunset), sunrise, sunset, the moon and solunar feeding times, and ⏰ Alarms: tick legal light start, sunrise, sunset or legal light end, pick how early, and the app rings and shows a notice while it's open; “Add to my calendar” puts the next 14 days at that spot in your phone's calendar with alerts, so they ring with the app closed.

    Map layers are grouped, and each one shows its own key when it's on; the Key button on the map shows the same key there. Only zones, parks and the scent cone are on at first, and every layer has its own colour. Boundaries: hunting zones, BC limited entry zones, and parks, towns and closures (red stripes no hunting, blue rules vary, green usually open, violet reserves, grey towns where shooting is banned by bylaw, black dots BC motor vehicle closures). Land: BC private parcels (ParcelMap BC, from zoom 11), Alberta grazing leases on public land and Alberta's Green Area (public forest). Habitat: forest types in five hunter classes (thick timber for bedding cover, open timber, aspen, birch and mixed forest, young forest and brush for browse, and meadows and grass; BC forest inventory, Alberta fuel types), old burns by age, and wetlands. Terrain: slope angle and aspect. Tools: the scent cone and 50 to 400 m range rings around the spot. Every layer works on the 3D map too.

    “Hunting maps offline” saves the zones, parks, seasons and draw results, and the picture layers you have on for the area on screen (zoom 10 to 15), so they work with no signal; save the base map with Offline maps (in the Ride tab on phones, the Layers menu on computers).

    Your points: truck or parking, tree stands, blinds, sightings, tracks, rubs, scrapes, bedding and feeding areas, trail cameras, water, harvests and camps, each with a photo and notes, on the flat and 3D maps (signed in, they go to your account too). Open one to Share it or “Guide me here”. A 🎯 Harvest point takes the species, animal, date and zone (filled in from the map), and the Hunting log lists harvests by season (April to March) with a CSV download. A 📷 Trail camera point keeps its checks: each time you pull the card, add the best photo, the date and what it caught, and the log shows every camera's photos in order. This is a guide, not the law: always check the BC Hunting and Trapping Regulations Synopsis or the Alberta Guide to Hunting Regulations.

Weather: Open-Meteo. Terrain: Mapzen Terrain Tiles on AWS.
